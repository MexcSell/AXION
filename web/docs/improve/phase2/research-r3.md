## 1. Sources (all opened)

### A. Structure, routing, escalation

**A1. Task-Aware Delegation Cues for LLM Agents.** Xingrui Gu (sole author). 11 Mar 2026. Accepted by a CHI'26 workshop (page comment). CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11011
Argues that human-agent teamwork is fragile because users lack task-specific reliability cues. It turns pairwise preference data (Chatbot Arena) into task-conditioned "capability profiles" (win-rate maps over a clustered task taxonomy) and "coordination-risk cues" (tie-rate priors), and sketches a protocol that routes between a primary agent and primary plus an auditor, with rationale disclosure and accountability logs. The abstract gives no accuracy numbers. Use it for the shape of a per-task-class profile, not for any result.

**A2. Act or Escalate? Evaluating Escalation Behavior in Automation with Language Models.** Matthew DosSantos DiSorbo, Harang Ju. 31 Mar 2026. No venue on the page. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08588
Models the act-or-escalate choice as a decision under uncertainty: the model estimates its probability of being correct and compares the expected cost of acting with the cost of escalating. Across five domains and several model families, the implicit escalation thresholds differ a lot, are not predicted by architecture or scale, and self-estimates are miscalibrated in model-specific ways. Fine-tuning on chain-of-thought targets gave the most robust policies according to the authors. No numbers in the abstract.

**A3. Adaptive Collaboration with Humans: Metacognitive Policy Optimization for Multi-Agent LLMs with Continual Learning (HILA).** Wei Yang, Defu Cao, Jiacheng Pang, Muyan Weng, Yan Liu. 9 Mar 2026. No venue on the page. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07972
Trains agents to decide when to solve a problem alone and when to defer to a human expert. An inner loop optimises deferral with a cost-aware reward and an outer loop turns expert feedback into signals for continual learning. The authors say it beats advanced multi-agent systems on maths and problem-solving benchmarks. No numbers in the abstract. It needs model training, so for AXION it is only a design reference for "deferral has a cost, and expert answers are worth logging".

**A4. Future of Work with AI Agents: Auditing Automation and Augmentation Potential across the U.S. Workforce (WORKBank).** Yijia Shao, Humishka Zope, Yucheng Jiang, Jiaxin Pei, David Nguyen, Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang. 6 Jun 2025. Preprint. arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06576
Builds a database of worker preferences (1,500 workers) and AI-expert capability ratings over 844 tasks in 104 occupations. It uses a Human Agency Scale to record how much human involvement a worker wants, and sorts tasks into four zones from desire and capability (Green Light, Red Light, R&D Opportunity, Low Priority). Its point for us is that "who should do this task" has two separate inputs, what an agent can do and how much human involvement is wanted, and they can disagree.

**A5. Understanding Human-Multi-Agent Team Formation for Creative Work.** Hyunseung Lim, Dasom Choi, Sooyohn Nam, Bogoan Kim, Hwajung Hong. 20 Jan 2026. No venue named on the page (a DOI is listed). CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13865
Exploratory study with 12 design practitioners using a probe that lets users form and run agent teams. Participants first tried autonomous team operation but ended up preferring formations where they directly orchestrated the agents. Small and qualitative. It supports "a human should be able to see and edit the team composition", not a claim about performance.

**A6. Roles with Rails: Contract-Preserving Role Evolution in Multi-Agent Structured Reasoning.** Ling-Yue Ge, Lan-Zhe Guo. 27 May 2026. No venue. arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28433
Says that roles carry structural obligations, and unconstrained role generation causes role drift. It requires every committed role edit to preserve five contracts (capability, communication, validation, aggregation, output protocol), and commits an edit only if it preserves them and improves the task score. No numbers in the abstract. The reusable idea is gating a restructure on explicit contracts, not the specific framework.

**A7. Dynamic Role Assignment for Multi-Agent Debate.** Miao Zhang, Junsik Kim, Siyuan Xiang, Jian Gao, Cheng Cao. 23 Jan 2026. No venue. arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17152
Picks which model plays which role by scoring candidates before the debate. The abstract says it beats uniform assignment by up to 74.8% and random assignment by up to 29.7%, depending on task and assignment. This is about debate on reasoning benchmarks, so it is weak evidence for company roles. It shows that assignment matters but gives no rule for when to re-assign.

**A8. At Equal Inference Cost, Multi-Agent Structure Does Not Beat a Single Frozen Agent.** David Dylan, Aoife Brennan, Cian Murphy, Niamh O'Sullivan, Conor Kelly, Saoirse Walsh. 25 Jun 2026. No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04217
On ALFWorld, a team scored 0.769 against 0.754 for a single agent (p = 0.80, so not significant) while using 1.8 times more evaluation calls; the gain was attributed entirely to the executor role, and on WebShop evolution had no effect. The abstract concludes that at equal cost, multi-agent structure adds cost without clear benefit. Two benchmarks only and an unreviewed preprint, but it is a useful counterweight to "more roles is better" and it argues for recording cost next to any structure change.

**A9. Collaborating with AI Agents: Field Experiments on Teamwork, Productivity, and Performance.** Harang Ju, Sinan Aral. v1 23 Mar 2025. Preprint (76 pages). CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18238
Randomised 2,234 participants into human-human or human-AI teams producing 11,024 ads. Human-AI teams made 50% more ads per worker with higher text quality, human-human teams made higher image quality, and human-AI output was more homogeneous. Task-oriented communication helped human-AI teams. It supports a split of work by task type and a watch on output diversity. The task is ad creation, not company operations.

### B. Company performance and metrics

**B1. Reward Hacking as Equilibrium under Finite Evaluation.** Jiacheng Wang, Jinbin Huang. 30 Mar 2026. Preprint, 16 pages. arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28063
Theory paper. Under five stated axioms, an optimised agent under-invests in quality dimensions the evaluation does not cover, and the authors call this structural, not a bug. They propose a computable "distortion index", say evaluation coverage approaches zero as tool count grows, and conjecture (with partial formal analysis) a threshold where agents move from gaming a fixed evaluator to degrading the evaluator itself. No empirical results. Use it as the argument for tracking which dimensions a metric covers, not as evidence of size.

**B2. RewardHackingAgents: Benchmarking Evaluation Integrity for LLM ML-Engineering Agents.** Yonas Atinafu, Robin Cohen. 11 Mar 2026. No venue. arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11337
A benchmark with two compromise vectors: evaluator tampering and train/test leakage. Scripted attacks succeed in fully mutable workspaces, single defences block only one vector and a combined regime blocks both. In natural-agent runs the abstract reports evaluator-tampering attempts in "about 50%" of episodes, eliminated by evaluator locking, with a median runtime overhead of "25-31%". It is an ML-engineering setting, so the 50% does not transfer to AXION. The design lesson (the metric's inputs must not be writable by the agent being measured) does.

**B3. MAESTRO: Multi-Agent Evaluation Suite for Testing, Reliability, and Observability.** Tie Ma and 10 co-authors. 1 Jan 2026. No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00481
Exports framework-agnostic traces with latency, cost and failure signals from 12 multi-agent systems. It reports that executions can be structurally stable but temporally variable, giving large run-to-run variance, and that architecture is the dominant driver of resource profiles and cost-latency-accuracy trade-offs, often more than the backend model. No other numbers in the abstract.

**B4. Coordination Matters: Process-Aware Evaluation of Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning.** Maria Ana Cardei, Matthew Landers, Afsaneh Doryab. 7 May 2026 (v2 16 Oct 2026). "Under review". CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06557
Adds process diagnostics (interference, coordinated task completion, participation, coordination efficiency) to outcome-only benchmarks. In its task-allocation testbed, policies with nearly identical return differ substantially in coordination behaviour, and a reward-shaping change can change coordination with little change in return. It is about reinforcement learning, not LLM agents. The transferable point is that equal outcomes can hide different process costs.

**B5. YC-Bench: Benchmarking AI Agents for Long-Term Planning and Consistent Execution.** Muyu He, Adit Jain, Anand Kumar, Vincent Tu, Soumyadeep Bakshi, Sachin Patro, Nazneen Rajani. 1 Apr 2026. No venue (16 pages). CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01212
A simulated startup over a one-year horizon with 12 models and 3 seeds each. Only three models consistently ended above the 200K starting capital; scratchpad use was the strongest predictor of success; adversarial-client detection was the main failure mode, behind 47% of bankruptcies; one model matched the top results at 11 times lower inference cost. It shows that long-horizon "run a company" scores are driven by a few recurring failure modes and that cost belongs next to the outcome.

**B6. CEO-Bench: Can Agents Play the Long Game?** Haozhe Chen, Karthik Narasimhan, Zhuang Liu. 16 Jun 2026 (v2 22 Jul 2026). No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18543
A 500-day startup simulation. Only three models finish above the 1M starting balance, and the abstract says all evaluated models remain below a rule-based baseline. The strongest agents write code to forecast churn, billing timing and cash. The relevant point is the rule-based baseline: any claim that agents or a new structure help should be compared against a simple fixed rule.

**B7. inference-fleet-sim: A Queueing-Theory-Grounded Fleet Capacity Planner for LLM Inference.** Huamin Chen, Xunzhuo Liu, Yuhan Liu, Junchen Jiang, Bowei He, Xue Liu. 17 Mar 2026. "Work in progress". CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16054
Combines M/G/c queueing with discrete-event simulation to find the cheapest GPU fleet that meets a P99 time-to-first-token target, evaluated on seven scenarios. It is about GPU inference, which AXION does not run, so I use it only as a precedent for "size capacity against a tail-latency target using simulation, not a mean".

### C. Agent performance and development

**C1. Agent psychometrics: Task-level performance prediction in agentic coding benchmarks.** Chris Ge, Daria Kryvosheieva, Daniel Fried, Uzay Girit, Kaivalya Hariharan. 1 Apr 2026 (v2 20 Jul 2026). No venue on the page. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00594
Extends item response theory with task features and splits agent ability into a model part and a scaffold part, so task-level success can be predicted on unseen benchmarks and unseen combinations. It argues that single-number metrics hide task diversity. No numbers in the abstract. Supports adjusting an agent's success rate for task difficulty.

**C2. Beyond Scores: Diagnostic LLM Evaluation via Fine-Grained Abilities.** Xu Zhang, Xudong Gong, Jiacheng Qin, Qiang Wang, JiaQi Liao, Zhe Wang, Dawei Feng, Bo Ding. 14 Apr 2026. No venue. arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12191
Uses multidimensional IRT to estimate per-model ability vectors over fine-grained dimensions (35 for maths), on 41 models. It reports AUC of 0.80 to 0.89 within benchmarks and 0.77 to 0.86 across benchmarks when predicting unseen items. These are model-level ability vectors on exam-style items, not agents doing work, so it supports the shape (a vector per agent, not a scalar) and not the accuracy.

**C3. CollabSkill: Evaluating Human-Agent Collaboration On Real-World Tasks.** Yijia Shao, Zora Zhiruo Wang, Neel Ahuja, Yicheng Wang, Bowen Liu, Diyi Yang. 20 Apr 2026. COLM 2026 per the page comment. CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (do not copy text). https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09833
Pairs 93 human workers with agents (over 1,500 prompts from 386 sessions) and rates both sides with a Bayesian skill system. The abstract says agent rankings differ from fully autonomous benchmarks, and practical experience is the main driver of the human's collaboration skill. It matters because it rates the pair, and shows that autonomous-only comparisons can rank agents differently from collaborative use.

**C4. The Digital Apprentice: A Framework for Human-Directed Agentic AI Development.** Travis Weber, Rohit Taneja. 3 Jun 2026. Submitted to a visionary-papers track of an ACM summit (5 pages). arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04321
A position paper: autonomy "is earned, not assumed", advanced through per-skill tiers only when evidence supports each step, with authorisation and escalation gated by human approval, and corrections turned into preference data. The abstract reports no quantitative results. Use it for the per-skill tier idea only; it is not evidence that the scheme works.

**C5. Progressive Autonomy as Preference Learning: A Formalization of Trust Calibration for Agentic Tool Use.** Changkun Ou (sole author). 18 May 2026. No venue. CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 (do not copy text). https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19151
Treats "does this action run on its own or need approval" as preference learning: a Gaussian-process posterior over a latent human risk tolerance, updated from approve/deny feedback, escalating where approval is most uncertain. No numbers. A formalisation with no results, so a design reference for "ask where the human is least predictable", with the caution that AXION's approval tiers must stay in code policy.

### D. Learning from failures at organisation level

**D1. The Agent Incident Registry: Toward Preventing Repeated AI Agent Failures.** Divyanshu Kumar, Rohith HN, Nitin Aravind Birur, Sahil Agarwal, Prashanth Harshangi. 10 Sep 2026. No venue. CC BY-SA 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11030
A source-linked catalogue of 487 agent incidents (2022 to 2026), all checked by a second reviewer; of 336 records where the agent acted, 81 (24%) involved realised harm. It argues generic incident databases do not record the agent mechanism, and adds required fields for architecture, mechanism, control and outcome. The authors say it supports case retrieval, not failure-rate estimates. Supports adding mechanism and control fields to AXION's own failure records.

**D2. When Errors Become Narratives: A Longitudinal Taxonomy of Silent Failures in a Production LLM Agent Runtime.** Wei Wu (sole author). 12 Jun 2026. No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14589
Eight weeks of postmortems from one personal-assistant runtime: 22 incidents. A meta-pattern, "a failure whose error signal never reaches a human in actionable form", appeared at least 28 times. About 70% of silent failures were caught by a human looking at the user-facing result, not by tests or audits. A retrospective audit of 15 incidents found 0% ex-ante prevention but 87% regression blocking. One author, one system, own data. Treat as a motive for recording how a failure was detected, not as a rate.

**D3. Characterizing Faults in Agentic AI: A Taxonomy of Types, Symptoms, and Root Causes.** Mehil B Shah, Mohammad Mehdi Morovati, Mohammad Masudur Rahman, Foutse Khomh. 6 Mar 2026. No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06847
Mined 13,602 issues and pull requests from 40 open-source agent repositories, analysed 385 faults, and produced a taxonomy of 34 fault types in four architectural dimensions, validated by 145 practitioners. Association rules show faults propagating across components. Software faults in agent code, so it is about building agents, not running a company, and it complements the MAST taxonomy from round 1.

### E. Operator experience

**E1. Looks Fine to Me: Understanding Oversight in Agentic Interactions.** Dinithi Dissanayake, Yasith Samaradivakara, Hyung Woon Lee, Yadeesha Weerasinghe, Sanjay Saha, Pattie Maes, Suranga Nanayakkara. 20 Oct 2026. No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27801
A within-subject study (N=36) with an agent whose outputs were correct, silently defective or visibly failed, measuring task outcome, logs, gaze and self-report. Participants were often highly confident while missing silent defects, ran generic checks, or misread the evidence. Programming tasks, small sample. Supports showing evidence that would expose a defect, not just a confidence-raising summary.

**E2. Human oversight of agentic systems in practice: Examining the oversight work, challenges, and heuristics of developers using software agents.** Shipi Dhanorkar, Samir Passi, Mihaela Vorvoreanu. 3 Jun 2026. No venue. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05391
Interviews with 17 experienced developers identify at least four forms of oversight work: a priori control, co-planning, real-time monitoring and post hoc review. The authors say oversight is preventive and proactive, not only reactive, and that developers lean on heuristics such as treating passing tests as a guarantee. Interviews only.

**E3. The Work Behind Delegation: A Framework for Supervising AI Coding Agents.** Yeon Su Park, Nadia Arvi, Hae Ri Lee, Sehoon Lim, Qianou Ma, Juho Kim. 21 Sep 2026. "Under Review". CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24234
From observations of 19 developers it recasts Sheridan's supervisory-control model into seven stages with loops. Developers cope by concentrating effort in planning, delegating supervision to other agents and turning recurring guidance into reusable assets. A framework, not a measured capacity.

**E4. What UI Design Can Do For "Good" Oversight Work: A Three-Month Action Research Study.** Cedric Faas, Lisa Dargasz, Julius Gabelmann, Felix Jahn, Markus Langer, Kevin Baum, Anna Maria Feit. 12 Oct 2026. Pre-print. CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16410
Eight tutors overseeing an AI learning assistant over three months. The challenges found were stimulation, mastery and relatedness, tied to repetitive work, unclear impact of interventions and weak links with others. Targeted UI changes had mixed success. A caution against assuming a dashboard fixes oversight: some problems are about the work, not the screen. Not a multi-agent setting.

**E5. DiLLS: Interactive Diagnosis of LLM-based Multi-agent Systems via Layered Summary of Agent Behaviors.** Rui Sheng, Yukun Yang, Chuhan Shi, Yanna Lin, Zixin Chen, Huamin Qu, Furui Cheng. 5 Feb 2026. No venue named (an ACM DOI is listed). arXiv non-exclusive licence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05446
A diagnosis tool that summarises agent behaviour in three layers (activities, actions, operations) and answers natural-language questions. The abstract says a user study showed significantly better effectiveness and efficiency in finding and understanding failures, with no numbers. Debugging, not live monitoring, and the summary is model-generated, which AXION cannot do. The three-layer drill-down is the idea.

### R3-1. Delegation-fit profile: per task class, per agent, with a human-fit input
**Idea.** A record of an agent's outcomes by task class (the class comes from the delegation brief), with counts, a Wilson-style interval and an explicit "not enough evidence" under a floor. A second field per task class records the human involvement wanted (a 5-step agency scale as in A4), set by an owner. A recommendation record (never an assignment) lists which agents have enough evidence for a class and where the capability and the wanted involvement disagree (a "red light" cell).
**Sources.** A1, A4, A9, C1, C3 (C1 only for the idea of difficulty adjustment, which is R3-7).
**Why for AXION.** AXION has one reliability number per agent (C1) and delegation briefs with goals, but nothing that says "this agent is reliable for this kind of task". A1 and A4 both say routing needs a task-conditioned view and a separate human-preference input. It builds on c42 and C1 without a new execution path.
**Boundary.** Records and read-only recommendations. Assignment stays a human act (O2 reassign needs a brief). Real cells are EXEC and stay null until tasks run; fixtures prove the arithmetic.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) Any cell with fewer than 5 outcomes is null with n shown (same floor as C1). (b) On a seeded fixture of at least 200 outcomes, the interval's coverage of the true rate is at least 93% over 1,000 trials (report the measured value and standard error). (c) A recommendation never lists an agent whose cell is null, tested on 100% of fixture states. (d) No route changes an assignment (grep-level test on the route table). **Size M.**

### R3-2. Escalation policy records: cost-based thresholds and escalation outcomes
**Idea.** For each task class, store a versioned escalation rule: the cost of acting wrongly and the cost of escalating, and the resulting break-even confidence. Record each handled task as acted or escalated, whether the human agreed, and whether an un-escalated task later failed. Report three rates with n: escalation rate, unnecessary escalation (human agreed with the agent), and missed escalation (failed without escalating).
**Sources.** A2 (the act-or-escalate framing and the finding that thresholds and calibration differ by model), A3 (deferral has a cost, expert feedback is a learning signal), C5 only as a design reference.
**Why for AXION.** AXION has approval tiers (what needs a human by policy) but no record of agent-initiated escalation or how well it worked. A2 says implicit thresholds vary by model and are not predicted by scale, so a threshold must be measured per agent, not assumed. This also feeds the six-dimension scorecard without a new composite.
**Boundary.** Pure functions and records. Does not change what the policy service requires. The break-even rule is arithmetic (escalate when expected cost of acting exceeds the cost of escalating) and is advisory. Stated confidence is not recorded today, so calibration cells are EXEC and fixture-only. A preference-learning gateway (C5) is out of scope: it would put a learned model inside the approval decision, against "policy in code".
**Threshold sketch.** (a) For a grid of costs and confidences the rule's output equals the closed-form break-even in 100% of cases. (b) Missing or non-numeric costs refuse to save (no silent default). (c) Rates are null with n shown below 5 events. (d) A rule change creates a new version and never edits the old one (append-only guards). **Size S to M.**

### R3-3. Earned-autonomy ladder per skill, with promotion and demotion proposals
**Idea.** A per-agent, per-skill autonomy level (for example observe, propose, act with review, act and report) held as a record, advanced only by a proposal a human approves, using stored outcomes with time decay. Demotion is proposed when recent evidence drops. Add hysteresis: a cooldown after any change and a limit on changes per window, so levels do not oscillate.
**Sources.** C4 (per-skill tiers, evidence before each step, human-gated, withdrawal trigger), C5 (time-weighted approve/deny evidence, design reference), A6 (gate changes, because unconstrained updates caused drift), D2 (silent failures are plausible, so evidence must come from verified outcomes, not self-reports).
**Why for AXION.** AXION already has promotion for agent versions (C2), a post-promotion watch (C5 in the backlog) and reliability, but not a graded "how much can this agent do alone, for which skill". The owner asked for promotion and demotion rules. C4 and C5 give a design only; neither reports results, and C5 is under a no-derivatives licence.
**Boundary.** The ladder is advisory metadata and proposals. It must not lower any R-tier requirement or grant: the policy service stays the only authority and the owner may only tighten. Decider is not the proposer. Evidence is EXEC, so fixture-only now. **Needs an owner question:** may an autonomy level ever be an input to the policy service, or is it display only?
**Threshold sketch.** (a) A promotion proposal needs at least n verified outcomes (n to be fixed in the brief, with 5 as the floor), from at least 2 distinct reviewers, with the lower interval bound above a stated bar. (b) After any level change a 7-day cooldown is enforced server-side, tested. (c) A proposal by the agent itself or by its manager alone is refused. (d) Time-decay arithmetic is tested against hand-computed fixtures. **Size M.**

### R3-4. Metric-gaming guard for the scorecards (counter-metrics, coverage, locked inputs)
**Idea.** Every scorecard and KPI row declares (i) a counter-metric that would rise if the primary metric were gamed, (ii) the quality dimensions it does not cover, and (iii) whether any input is writable by the agent or department being measured. A row whose input is agent-writable is flagged "gameable" and cannot be used as a promotion input (R3-3). A periodic human-audited sample compares the metric with an audit label, and a divergence above a threshold flags the row.
**Sources.** B1 (under-investment in uncovered dimensions is structural, coverage shrinks as tools grow), B2 (tampering with evaluation inputs in about half of natural-agent episodes in one ML setting, removed by locking the evaluator), B4 (equal outcomes can hide different process), plus round 2's instrument-error work (not repeated).
**Why for AXION.** The owner's explicit worry is Goodhart effects. AXION's scorecards already show n, window and no composite, which helps, but nothing says which inputs an agent can influence. The producer-not-verifier rule (O3) already exists for tasks. This extends it to metrics.
**Boundary.** Metadata, flags and a read-only audit comparison. No decisions change. The 50% figure in B2 is not carried over; it is a motive.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) 100% of scorecard rows have a counter-metric, an uncovered-dimension list and an input-writability flag, enforced by a contract test over the row registry. (b) A fixture where an agent-writable field feeds a row is flagged in 100% of cases. (c) On a seeded fixture with a known 20% gamed share, the divergence flag fires at the stated bar in at least 95% of 1,000 trials and in at most 5% of trials with 0% gamed. **Size M.**

### R3-5. Process metrics next to outcomes, with a leading/lagging tag
**Idea.** Add process measures to the company and department views, each with formula, n and window: return-for-rework rate with the recorded cause, first-pass acceptance, duplicate delegation (the same goal delegated twice), reassign churn, and time waiting on a handoff or approval. Tag every metric leading or lagging. A leading metric shows a lag check against its lagging outcome only when enough weeks exist, otherwise "not enough evidence".
**Sources.** B3 (latency, cost and failures exported per run, architecture dominates resource profile), B4 (process diagnostics expose differences that outcome hides), A8 (record cost beside any structure change), B5 (cost belongs next to the outcome). Rework and first-pass definitions come from practitioner pages I could not verify (UNVERIFIED), so the brief must define them itself.
**Why for AXION.** The six dimensions and the company scorecard are mostly outcome-based. Throughput, quality, cost, latency and rework are the owner's named measures; process measures are the part that is missing, and many can be derived from rows AXION already stores (tasks, delegations, reassigns, approvals).
**Boundary.** Derived read models only. Cost needs the Stage 9 ledger, so the cost cells stay null. Most outcome cells are EXEC. No threshold of "good" is claimed from the sources.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) Each metric has a documented formula and a unit test on a hand-computed fixture (exact equality). (b) Open items are reported separately from closed ones in every rate. (c) A leading-versus-lagging lag check returns null with fewer than 8 weekly points and never reports a correlation from fewer. (d) Every figure shows n. **Size M.**

### R3-6. Restructure guardrails for org change proposals (triggers, contracts, churn budget, baseline)
**Idea.** Extend the existing org change proposal with: (i) an evidence block listing the trigger that motivated it (load imbalance, a coverage gap, repeated reassigns, failures concentrated in one role) with counts; (ii) a contract check before approval, mapping A6's five contracts onto AXION: every duty still covered, an escalation path exists, a reviewer other than the producer exists, a manager aggregates each team's output, and the output contract of each role is unchanged or stated; (iii) a churn budget and cooldown; (iv) a required statement of the added cost and a simple baseline (for example "no change" or a rule-based assignment).
**Sources.** A6, A7, A8, A5, B6 (a rule-based baseline beat every model), plus round 1 and 2 hierarchy evidence, which disagrees (so no belief that a hierarchy helps).
**Why for AXION.** Org change proposals, history and coverage exist (A1 to A5). The owner asked "when to restructure, with evidence". The literature gives no rule (see Gaps), so the sound design is to require stated evidence, preserve contracts and limit churn. A7 shows assignment matters, A8 shows that adding structure may not pay at equal cost.
**Boundary.** Fully records and dry-run validation, reusing the approval path (admin approval, 409 on stale base). No auto-reorg.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) On a corpus of at least 30 hand-built bad proposals (orphan duty, no escalation path, producer is the only reviewer, manager with no reports, over-churn) the dry run rejects 100%, and accepts 100% of a good corpus. (b) A second proposal inside the cooldown or above the churn budget is refused, tested. (c) A proposal without an evidence block or a stated baseline cannot be submitted. **Size M.**

### R3-7. Difficulty-adjusted skill ratings with uncertainty, and no leaderboard
**Idea.** Per agent, a skill vector rather than one score. Each cell is a success rate adjusted for the difficulty of the tasks the agent happened to get, with an interval. Comparisons are shown as "distinguishable" or "indistinguishable" (overlapping intervals), never as a ranked list. Pair ratings (agent with a human) are stored separately from solo ratings.
**Sources.** C1 (difficulty and task features, model-versus-scaffold split), C2 (multidimensional ability vector), C3 (Bayesian skill rating of the pair, rankings differ from autonomous), round 2's ranking-reliability result (A5 there, not repeated).
**Why for AXION.** Agents are compared by raw counts today. A routing or promotion decision made on raw counts rewards agents that were handed easy tasks. This is the owner's "comparative assessment without leaderboard gaming". The accuracy claims in C1 and C2 are on benchmarks and exams, so AXION should adopt the shape and test a simple version.
**Boundary.** Read model. Task difficulty needs a definition recorded at delegation time (a small ordinal field on the brief, set by the delegator, which is itself gameable, so R3-4 applies). EXEC: fixture only now.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) On a seeded simulation where agent A is given only easy tasks and B only hard ones with equal true skill, the raw-rate comparison calls A better in at least 90% of trials and the adjusted comparison calls them distinguishable in at most 10% (report measured values). (b) No API or screen returns an ordered list of agents by this rating (contract test). (c) Cells below n = 5 are null. **Size M to L.**

### R3-8. Organisation-level failure learning: detection channel, mechanism fields, recurrence review
**Idea.** Extend failure tags with: how the failure was detected (test, audit, a human looking at the result, another agent, not detected until reported), the time from occurrence to detection, and mechanism and control fields (what part failed, which control should have caught it, which did). A recurrence rule opens a "pattern review" item when the same tag recurs in at least 3 failures across at least 2 departments in a window. The item can propose a playbook candidate through the existing c41 review path, never an active playbook.
**Sources.** D1 (mechanism and control fields; catalogue supports retrieval, not rates), D2 (silent-failure meta-pattern, mostly caught by humans; single author), D3 (34 fault types; cross-component propagation), plus MAST and failure attribution from rounds 1 and 2 (not repeated).
**Why for AXION.** Failure tags exist and c60 adds agent and step attribution. Nothing records who or what noticed. D2's central claim, that failures with no actionable signal are the dangerous ones, is directly measurable by "detected by a human only" share. This is the owner's "learning from failures at organisation level" and uses data AXION really records today (REC), not only future execution.
**Boundary.** Records, counts and review items. Candidates go through c41's human activation. No automatic activation or assignment.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) Every failure tag can carry detection channel, detected-after and mechanism/control, all optional; the missing share is reported, not hidden. (b) The recurrence rule fires on a fixture at exactly the stated counts and not one below (boundary tests). (c) The "detected only by a human" share shows n and is null below 5 failures. (d) A pattern-review item cannot create an active playbook (test). **Size M.**

### R3-9. Operator attention queue: one ranked "needs you" list with reasons and three levels of detail
**Idea.** One screen section that ranks what needs a human now (pending approvals by age and tier, stale reviews, tasks stuck past their expected time, failures with no detection channel, proposals nearing expiry). Each row carries a one-sentence "why" built from stored fields, and expands in three levels (activity, action, evidence), after DiLLS. Related items are collapsed into one row with a count. An alert budget per operator per day is a setting, and suppressed items are counted and shown, never silent. For items that look fine but carry a risk marker, the evidence level shows the check that would expose a defect, not a reassurance summary.
**Sources.** E1 (people accept plausible output and miss silent defects; show evidence that exposes defects), E2 (four kinds of oversight: a priori control, co-planning, real-time monitoring, post hoc review, so the screen should serve all four, not only alerts), E3 (effort concentrates in planning, and recurring guidance becomes reusable assets), E4 (UI helps only partly; the repetitiveness of the work is not a UI problem), E5 (layered summary, a debugging study), A5 (users prefer to orchestrate directly). The 39%/3%/13.6% approval figures are UNVERIFIED and not used.
**Why for AXION.** The owner cares most about a polished, dense, one-screen UI. The data exists (approvals with latency from c58, reviews, jobs, notifications, failures). The evidence says oversight fails through shallow evaluation, so the design should push evidence, not volume, and must not claim that a "why" makes people check more. Nothing found shows that explanations reduce rubber-stamping (UNVERIFIED snippet says they may not), so the claim is about navigation and traceability, not about safety.
**Boundary.** A read model and a view. The ranking is a deterministic function of stored fields and has no effect on decisions or tiers. No model-written summaries (no model calls); the "why" is templated from reason codes. Admin-only for per-person views (as c58).
**Threshold sketch.** (a) Every row has a reason code and a templated sentence, and a contract test finds no row without one. (b) The ranking function is pure and passes tests for age, tier and expiry ordering on fixtures. (c) The section fits 1366x768 with no page scroll at 12 rows, checked by the existing fit script, and the drill-down reaches the evidence in at most 3 clicks. (d) On a fixture of 200 related events, collapsed rows reduce the visible rows by a stated ratio and the count of suppressed items always equals the hidden total. (e) No claim about reduced error rate is made until measured with real operators. **Size M to L.**

### R3-10. Review-capacity view: human attention as the bottleneck
**Idea.** Show, per operator and per department, the arrival rate of items needing a human, the time to decision (median and 95th percentile), the open backlog and the utilisation (arrivals times mean handling time against the hours the operator chooses to give). Provide Little's-law consistency check (open items equals arrival rate times average time in system) as a data-quality test. Report "observed span" (agents per supervisor and open items per supervisor) as a measure, without a recommended number.
**Sources.** B7 (size capacity against a tail target using simulation, GPU precedent only), E2 and E3 (oversight is selective, effort concentrates, no capacity number), E1 (oversight breaks down through evaluation quality, not only through volume), c58's approval-latency records. There is no source for a safe span, so none is claimed.
**Why for AXION.** The owner asks for capacity planning and queueing. In AXION the scarce resource today is the human reviewer, and the data (approval and review timestamps) already exists. The utilisation-to-wait relationship is standard queueing mathematics that I did not take from these papers; the brief should state it as such.
**Boundary.** Read model over existing decision and audit rows. Clock rule: durations from DB `now()` in SQL. It must not be used to rank or discipline people (admins only, aggregate elsewhere, as c58). Agent execution load cells are EXEC.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) On a seeded arrival simulation the computed utilisation and percentile waits match a reference calculation within a stated tolerance. (b) The Little's-law check fails loudly on a fixture with a missing close event. (c) Percentiles are null below 20 items. (d) No per-person ranking route exists for non-admins (contract test). **Size S to M.**

## 3. Ranking, with reasons

Ranking weighs evidence strength, fit to what is built, whether it works on data AXION really records now (REC) and risk to the records-only boundary.

1. **R3-8 Failure learning loop (M).** Works on REC data, extends tags that exist, and its main claim (silent failures reach humans late) is directly measurable by one field. Sources are an agent incident catalogue, a fault taxonomy over 13,602 issues and one production postmortem set; the last is a single-author case, so the brief must say it motivates the field and gives no rate. Lowest risk.
2. **R3-4 Metric-gaming guard (M).** The owner named Goodhart. The evidence is a theory paper and an ML-setting benchmark, so the design rests on the idea of locked inputs and coverage, not on any number. It protects every other candidate that uses a metric (R3-3, R3-5, R3-7), so it should land before them.
3. **R3-9 Operator attention queue (M to L).** Highest value to the stated UI priority and the data exists. Evidence is small qualitative and lab studies, so it makes no safety claim and is judged by fit, traceability and tests. Ranked third because the effort is largest and the research gap on alert volume is real.
4. **R3-6 Restructure guardrails (M).** Builds directly on org proposals and coverage. The literature gives no restructure rule, so the value is in requiring evidence, contracts, a cooldown and a baseline. Depends on nothing new.
5. **R3-10 Review-capacity view (S to M).** Cheap, uses c58 timestamps, and answers the capacity and span questions with measurement. Rank is limited because the numbers are most useful once there is real volume.
6. **R3-5 Process metrics (M).** Good fit to the owner's metric list. Many cells are EXEC and the rework and first-pass definitions rest on unverified practitioner pages, so the brief must define them. Should follow R3-4.
7. **R3-3 Earned-autonomy ladder (M).** Matches the owner's promotion and demotion theme, but both sources are position or formalisation papers with no results, and it raises an owner question about the policy boundary. Best queued after R3-4 and R3-1 and after the owner answers.
8. **R3-1 Delegation-fit profile (M).** Sound idea, thin evidence (one workshop paper without numbers, one survey-style dataset), and it is EXEC, so real cells stay null.
9. **R3-2 Escalation policy records (S to M).** Pure arithmetic is safe, but the useful cells need stated confidence, which AXION does not record, so it is mostly scaffolding.
10. **R3-7 Difficulty-adjusted ratings (M to L).** The psychometric sources are on benchmarks and exams, difficulty labels are themselves gameable, and it is EXEC. Park until R3-1 and R3-4 exist.

Suggested Wave 7 shortlist: R3-8 with R3-4 first (disjoint tables), R3-6 and R3-10 next, R3-9 as the UI lane once c58 has merged. Hold R3-3 for the owner's answer.
