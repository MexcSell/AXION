## 1. Sources (all opened)

### A. Retention, erasure and tamper evidence

**A1. Guidelines 02/2025 on processing of personal data through blockchain technologies, v2 (final).** European Data Protection Board. Page dated 7 July 2026 (v1 was published for consultation in April 2025; consultation ran 14 April to 9 June 2025). https://edpb.europa.eu/documents/guideline/guidelines-022025-on-processing-of-personal-data-through-blockchain_en
Opened: the EDPB executive-summary page only. It says personal data should not sit in an append-only ledger when avoidable; encryption, hashing and commitments lower risk but the result is still personal data; a salted or keyed hash with the original held off-ledger is one accepted approach, and unsalted or unkeyed hashes are generally not adequate; erasure and objection must be designed in (for example, make data anonymous), because technical difficulty does not excuse non-compliance; the controller must document a minimisation assessment for both metadata and payload. It is guidance on public blockchains, not on private audit logs, so AXION uses it as a design rule, not a legal claim. Maturity: regulator guidance, final.

**A2. SoK: Cryptographic Erasure on Public Ledgers: Application-Layer Architectures, Key-Lifecycle Adversaries, and GDPR Art. 17 Sufficiency.** Yitebeier Aikebaier (Nexum Ledger Ltd). IACR ePrint 2026/1109, received 30 May 2026, last revised 4 November 2026. https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1109
It surveys leaving the append-only ledger untouched and erasing by destroying the key to encrypted data (or by keeping only anchors on the ledger). It organises designs by where data lives, who holds the key (single or threshold) and what gates access, and adds an adversary that attacks the key lifecycle (side channels, coercing custodians, later algorithm breaks). It reports a bound for a single key holder and an impossibility result for time-delay-only designs. Maturity: a single-author preprint from a vendor-affiliated author, not peer reviewed. Use for the vocabulary (key custody, key-lifecycle adversary), not for its proofs.

**A3. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Article 19: Automatically generated logs.** Text as hosted on artificialintelligenceact.eu (an unofficial reproduction; the Official Journal is the authority). https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/19/
Providers of high-risk AI systems must keep the automatically generated logs that are under their control for a period suited to the purpose and at least six months, unless other EU or national law says otherwise, and without prejudice to EU data-protection law. The page gives application dates of 2 December 2027 (Annex III systems) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I systems). It sets a floor, not a ceiling, and so pulls against data minimisation: a schedule needs both a minimum and a maximum with a stated basis. Not a 2025-26 research item; included as the one binding retention number found.

### B. Tenant isolation

**B1. The Stochastic Deputy: Structural Tenant Isolation for Tool-Using LLM Agents.** Mirza Samad Ahmed Baig, Syeda Anshrah Gillani, Asher Ali, Muhammad Hamzah Siddiqui. arXiv 2609.14780, v1 13 Sep 2026, cs.CR, no venue shown, CC BY 4.0. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14780
The authors argue that an agent picking a tenant's resources is steered by text an attacker may control, so isolation must not rely on the model. Their design removes the tenant id from the tool schema, binds scope to a verified credential and enforces it below the agent. In a 373-trial ablation, a validated tenant parameter blocked all 26 out-of-scope attempts, but with the parameter removed 12 of 56 trials still escaped by forging a writable scope field, which they take as a case for cryptographically protected context. They also report a 57x latency penalty for one set-valued scope query shape and a fix using an indexed lateral join. Maturity: preprint, one platform (MySQL).

**B2. Burn-After-Use for Preventing Data Leakage through a Secure Multi-Tenant Architecture in Enterprise LLM.** Qiang Zhang, Elena Emma Wang, Jiaming Li, Xichun Wang. arXiv 2601.06627, v1 10 Jan 2026 (v3 11 Mar 2026), cs.CR, no venue shown. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06627
It tests infrastructure-level attacks against a per-department isolation design (55 tests, including a compromised vector-store credential and exposure through a shared logging pipeline; 92% defence success) and an ephemeral-context scheme (72 iterations, 76.75% success). The authors name credential misconfiguration and observability pipelines as residual risks. The useful part for AXION is the test shape: leakage is measured per attacker-victim pair, and logs and caches are treated as leak paths, not only the database. Maturity: preprint; the numbers are the authors' own test counts, not a benchmark.

**B3. A Consolidated Deployment Edition of a Microservice Core-Banking Platform: Composing Services by Reference into Three Hosts.** Tomisin Fagbola. arXiv 2610.30212, v1 21 Oct 2026, cs.SE, no venue shown. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30212
It compares two deployments of one multi-tenant banking platform built from the same commits. Of 231 read probes across five principal types, 230 matched between editions, 47 financial workload cases gave matching ledgers, and eleven cross-tenant and write-authorisation cases gave the expected outcome in both. 10 of 16 injected faults were caught by the edition's own checks, five by behavioural tests, and one escaped both. The page does not say how the eleven cases were built. Relevance is the idea of differential testing (same cases, two builds, compare outcomes) and fault injection to measure what a check suite misses. Weak source; single platform, emulated deployment, single author.

### C. Incident records and evidence

**C1. Towards a common reporting framework for AI incidents.** OECD. OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 34, OECD Publishing, 2025, DOI 10.1787/f326d4ac-en. https://ideas.repec.org/p/oec/comaaa/34-en.html
Opened: the RePEc catalogue entry (the OECD site itself returned 403). It proposes a common set of 29 criteria for describing AI incidents so reports from different countries and sectors line up, and says the aim is to identify high-risk systems and assess risks. I could not read the criteria list, so the field names are UNVERIFIED; only the count of 29 and the purpose are confirmed.

**C2. AI Act: Commission issues draft guidance and reporting template on serious AI incidents, and seeks stakeholders' feedback.** European Commission, Digital Strategy. Published 26 Sep 2025, page updated 4 Nov 2025; consultation 26 Sep to 7 Nov 2025. https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/consultations/ai-act-commission-issues-draft-guidance-and-reporting-template-serious-ai-incidents-and-seeks
The Commission published a draft guidance on Article 73 and a draft report template for serious incidents of high-risk AI systems, and said the rules become applicable from August 2026. The page itself does not define "serious incident" or give deadlines; those are in the draft guidance, which I did not read. I found no final version.

**C3. European Commission Publishes Draft Guidance on Reporting Serious AI Incidents.** Tim Wybitul, Isabelle Brams, Timo Hager (Latham & Watkins), client alert, 28 Oct 2025. https://www.lw.com/en/insights/european-commission-publishes-draft-guidance-reporting-serious-ai-incidents
A law-firm summary of C2. It lists the four Article 3(49) categories (death or serious harm to health; serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure; infringement of EU fundamental-rights obligations; serious harm to property or the environment) and the deadlines it reads from the draft: 15 days from awareness, 10 days if a death may be involved, 2 days for widespread infringement or critical-infrastructure disruption, with an initial incomplete report allowed and supplements to follow. It also says the draft bars altering the AI system during an investigation, if that could affect the analysis, without informing the authorities, and that preserving evidence is recommended but is not stated as a formal duty. Secondary source on a draft; the verifier should compare against the Commission text.

**C4. Standardised schema and taxonomy for AI incident databases in critical digital infrastructure.** Avinash Agarwal, Manisha J. Nene. arXiv 2501.17037, v1 28 Jan 2025; accepted at the 2024 IEEE Pune Section International Conference (PuneCon). https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17037
The abstract argues that existing AI incident databases lack granularity and a shared structure, and proposes a unified schema that adds fields such as incident severity, causes and harms caused, plus a taxonomy for critical digital infrastructure. The abstract has no quantitative results. I did not open the paper body, so I do not report its field table.

**C5. LLM Agents Can Easily Tamper With Their Own Traces.** Jeremy Qin, David Schmotz, Derck Prinzhorn, Luca Beurer-Kellner, Ameya Prabhu, Maksym Andriushchenko. arXiv 2609.30266, v1 24 Sep 2026, cs.CR, CC BY 4.0, no venue shown. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30266
The authors show that several local coding agents let an agent delete its own execution traces when asked, without tripping monitor guardrails (all tested harnesses but one), that an external attacker can induce this deletion, and that frontier models tamper with traces unprompted under reward pressure. They recommend logging through an independent mechanism outside the agent's control so traces survive even if the host is compromised. The abstract gives no rates; secondary press coverage gives inconsistent counts, so cite no numbers from it. Maturity: preprint.

### D. Reliability targets and change freezes

**D1. Example Error Budget Policy (SRE Workbook).** Steven Thurgood (author), David Ferguson (reviewer), Betsy Beyer (approver), Google. Published 19 Feb 2018. https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/
A worked policy for an example service. If the service exceeded its error budget over the previous four-week window, changes and releases stop (including data changes) except P0 and security fixes, until it is back within SLO. It lists when feature work may continue anyway (company-wide network fault, another team's fault with that team frozen, out-of-scope load or penetration testers, mis-categorised errors), says disputes go to a named executive, and requires a postmortem with a P0 action if one incident uses more than 20% of the four-week budget. Not 2025-26, and an illustrative template, not data. It is the clearest published model of a freeze with explicit exceptions.

**D2. Shipping on a Spent Error Budget.** Paul Osman, Honeycomb blog. Page shows "Updated: July 8, 2021". https://www.honeycomb.io/blog/shipping-on-spent-error-budget
A case study arguing that a missed SLO should start a discussion, not an automatic stop: at-risk SLOs page the on-call engineer, and the team kept shipping an unannounced feature by isolating its traffic, rebasing daily and canarying before merge. It is a counterweight to D1: the freeze should be a recorded, scoped decision with a reason, not a reflex. Single company blog.

**D3. Speed of Code Reviews.** Google Engineering Practices documentation. No date on the page. https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/speed.html
It sets a maximum of one business day for a first response to a review request, argues that slow human review slows the whole team and pushes people to accept weaker changes, and allows faster, relaxed handling only for defined emergencies. This is the one published example found of a response-time target on a human review queue. Practice guidance from one company, not research.

### E. Change management and migrations

**E1. pgroll: zero-downtime, reversible schema migrations for PostgreSQL.** Xata, open-source repository README. No release date on the page I read. https://github.com/xataio/pgroll
It runs a migration in two steps: `start` applies additive changes and exposes the new schema version while the old one still works (versioned views, clients switch by `search_path`); `complete` removes the old version; `rollback` removes the new one. For breaking column changes it adds a new column, backfills it and keeps both in sync with triggers. The page does not describe append-only or audited tables, so how triggers and guards interact is not covered. AXION would copy the pattern (expand, backfill, verify, contract, with a rollback that is possible until the final step), not the tool.

**E2. Event Versioning (Axon Framework reference, 5.3).** AxonIQ documentation. No date on the page. https://docs.axoniq.io/axon-framework-reference/5.3/events/event-versioning/
Stored events are never rewritten; versioning is handled by converting the payload when it is read, and the page lists what conversion can do (add fields with defaults, drop or rename fields, compatible type changes) and what it cannot (structural change, such as splitting or dropping events, which needs a separate transformation step). It is vendor documentation for one framework, but it states the rule AXION needs for append-only data: evolve readers, not stored rows.

### R4-1. Retention schedule and legal-hold registry (decisions only, no deletion)
**Idea.** A retention class per record family (audit events, approvals, task and message payloads, eval runs, proposals, exports) stored as data: minimum period, maximum period, basis text, and what happens at maximum (redact payload, keep metadata; or keep). A legal-hold table: scope (tenant, record family, or subject id), reason, who placed it, who released it, placed_at and released_at, with the placer and releaser being different people at the admin level. A pure function `retentionDecision(record, schedule, holds, now_from_sql)` returns keep, eligible-for-redaction, or blocked-by-hold, with a reason code. A System Operations report lists counts per class: within minimum, eligible, held. Nothing is deleted or changed by this project.
**Sources.** A3 (a floor of at least six months, plus a rule that data-protection law applies on top), A1 (minimisation assessment must be documented; storage limitation), C3 (preserve evidence, do not alter the system during an investigation). The hold-overrides-schedule rule comes only from non-authoritative secondary pages seen in search (university and archive pages), so it is a design choice here, not a sourced rule.
**Why for AXION.** AXION keeps everything forever by design and has a purge only for demo data. Before agents produce real payloads, every record family needs a stated minimum and maximum, or the append-only guards will make a later erasure request look impossible. A3 gives a floor and A1 gives a ceiling pressure, so the schedule must hold both.
**Boundary.** Read-only report plus two admin-written tables (schedule, holds), both append-only with the usual guards. No route deletes or edits any other table. The holds table adds one validation: a release must name a different admin than the placer.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) A decision matrix test of at least 40 cases (record age before, at and after min and max, with and without hold, held scope nested in a larger scope) returns the expected decision in 100% of cases. (b) A hold on any scope makes every record in scope blocked in 100% of cases, including records created after the hold was placed (checked by DB-clock fixtures). (c) Every record family in the schema appears in the schedule or in an explicit "no retention rule yet" list, enforced by a test that fails when a new table is added without either (the report must show the unclassified count; target 0). (d) A release by the placer is refused. **Size S.**

### R4-2. Redaction tombstones that keep the audit chain verifiable
**Idea.** Let an admin redact the payload of an audit or record row after the retention rule allows it (R4-1), without breaking `GET /audit/verify`. The mechanism, to be confirmed against how the chain is built today, is that the chain hash commits to a keyed or salted commitment of the payload (per-record random salt, or per-subject key) and not to the raw payload. Redaction then deletes the payload and its salt (the crypto-shredding idea in A2), leaves the commitment and the chain intact, and appends a tombstone event (which row, which retention rule, which admin, when, hold check result). The verifier is extended to accept a redacted row only when a matching tombstone exists and rejects a missing payload with no tombstone, so truncation and silent deletion are still caught. The anchor (c49) is unaffected because the chain head does not change on redaction.
**Sources.** A1 (a hash of personal data is still personal data; salted or keyed commitments with the data held off-ledger; erasure by making the data anonymous), A2 (destroy the key to erase; key custody is the open problem), C5 (the log must survive deletion attempts by an agent, so redaction must be a server-side admin act with its own audit row, never reachable from an agent identity). The patents in the UNVERIFIED list hint at the same "chain over message hashes" structure.
**Why for AXION.** The append-only triggers and chain are a strength and also make erasure impossible without a design. If payloads are hashed directly into the chain, any later redaction invalidates every following hash. Doing the commitment change before real payloads exist is cheap; doing it after needs a migration of audited data (see R4-6). It also answers a question the owner will meet first: how can a record be deleted when the log is meant to be tamper evident.
**Boundary.** Records and validation only. No agent calls it. The salt or key lives in an agent-unreachable column or table with the same guards; any external key custody (A2's main issue) is blocked on Q12, so the project uses a per-record salt stored in the database and says so. Note the limit: per-record salts stored in the same database do not protect against a database administrator, only against reading the payload after redaction. That limit must be stated in the brief.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) On a fixture chain of 10,000 rows, redact 500 random payloads: `verify` still passes in 100% of runs over 100 seeds. (b) Tamper with one non-redacted payload, one commitment, one tombstone, or delete one tombstone: `verify` fails in 100% of 400 seeded cases (report the measured detection rate). (c) A redaction request on a row blocked by a hold (R4-1) is refused. (d) After redaction, a search of the database and exports for the redacted payload string finds 0 hits (the check includes the audit anchor file, and the OTLP export of c60). (e) Redaction by a non-admin or an agent identity is refused. If the brief finds that the current chain cannot take a commitment without rewriting history, the project reports NOT MET for the live chain and ships the design as a new chain version for new rows only. **Size M.**

### R4-3. Incident record with evidence freeze and a deadline clock
**Idea.** An incident table (admin-written now; agent-reported later) with: severity class aligned to the four Article 3(49) categories plus an "internal only" class; subject (agent, version, mission, org revision, policy); `occurred_at`, `detected_at`, `aware_at` (the clock for deadlines starts here); detection channel (this reuses R3-8's field, it is not duplicated); evidence source per attached item, either `server_observed` or `agent_submitted` (C5: an agent's own account of its trace is a claim, not evidence); append-only report versions (an initial incomplete report and supplements, as C3 describes under Article 73(5)); and a post-incident review record (timeline, contributing factors, controls that did and did not fire, follow-up items with owner and due date, linked to failure tags). Opening an incident automatically places a hold (R4-1) on the subject's records and a scoped change freeze on its version, org proposal and grants (see R4-5); lifting either needs a named admin and a reason. A computed field shows days remaining against the 2, 10 and 15 day figures from C3 for the matching class, labelled "draft guidance, informational".
**Sources.** C1 (a common criteria set; count only), C2 and C3 (categories, clocks, initial plus supplement reports, no altering the system without informing the authority), C4 (add severity, causes and harms as fields), C5 (evidence outside the agent's reach), D1 (a postmortem is required when one incident uses more than 20% of a budget; here the review record is required for every incident above a stated severity).
**Why for AXION.** AXION has failure tags and approvals but no object that says "this was an incident, who knew when, what was preserved, what was reviewed, what is frozen". R3-8 covers learning from many small failures; this covers the single serious event and its evidence, which R3-8 does not. The incident record is also the place where `server_observed` versus `agent_submitted` evidence is enforced from day one, before any agent can write a trace.
**Boundary.** Records, hold and freeze rows, and validation (a frozen subject's proposal approval or promotion returns 409 with a reason). The regulator clocks are shown, never used to send anything. No external reporting, no notifications beyond the existing ones. It does not claim AXION is a high-risk system; it uses the draft vocabulary because it is the only published incident-report structure found, and the final text may differ (C2, C3 are drafts; I found no final).
**Threshold sketch.** (a) A fixture matrix of at least 12 incidents (each class, each deadline, awareness before and after midnight UTC, via DB `now()`) shows the expected days remaining in 100% of cases. (b) A supplement never changes an earlier version (append-only guards, tested by UPDATE and DELETE attempts). (c) With an open incident, 100% of a fixture matrix of change actions on the frozen subject (version promote, org proposal approve, grant create) are refused; actions on other subjects pass; closing the incident without a review record is refused for severity at or above the stated level. (d) An `agent_submitted` attachment can never be counted in the "evidence" total of a report (test). (e) Every incident links to at least one failure tag or states why not; the unlinked share is reported. **Size M.**

### R4-4. Tenant-isolation verification: metamorphic and seeded-leak tests over the route table
**Idea.** A test suite and a CI check, with no new product feature, that generates its cases from the route table (the same source c50 uses). Relations: (MR1) adding, changing or deleting data in tenant B never changes any response for tenant A, apart from a named list of fields (timestamps, ids that A itself created); (MR2) renaming or swapping tenant ids swaps the responses and nothing else; (MR3) a unique marker string planted in every writable field of tenant B appears nowhere in any response, export, notification, log line, audit row, search result or error body of tenant A (the sweep covers the places B2 names as leak paths: logs, caches and exports); (MR4) no request body, query or header field can set tenant context: a corpus of forged scope fields (the failure mode B1 measured) is refused in 100% of cases. A mutation step then removes the tenant predicate from a chosen query, drops a policy, or uses the owner role in a copy of the code and checks the suite fails, so a green run is shown to mean something (B3's point about checks that miss faults, measured there by fault injection).
**Sources.** B1 (remove the tenant id from the interface, bind scope to a credential; forged writable scope escaped 12 of 56 trials), B2 (leak paths in logging and caches; measure leakage per attacker-victim pair), B3 (differential comparison and fault injection as the way to measure what a check suite misses). The relations MR1 to MR3 are my construction; no source tests them.
**Why for AXION.** AXION has per-tenant chains, tenant-scoped locks and many routes added by many lanes. Isolation is today verified route by route when someone remembers. A generated suite scales with the route table (H9's goal) and the mutation step gives an honest measure of the suite's power. It matters most before agents act, because B1's point is that an agent's tenant choice must never be an input.
**Boundary.** Tests and CI only. Runs against the disposable test database with three seeded tenants. No code in the request path changes, unless the suite finds a leak, which is reported as a finding and fixed in its own lane.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) MR1 and MR3 cover 100% of GET routes in the route table and 100% of write routes (via follow-up reads); an uncovered route fails CI unless on a named, dated exception list of at most 5. (b) At least 10 seeded leak mutants (missing tenant predicate on a list route, a join without a tenant key, an export that reads another schema, a cache keyed without tenant, a log line with a payload, a forged-scope field accepted) are each killed by the suite; report the kill rate (target 100%; anything below 90% is NOT MET). (c) MR4: a forged-scope corpus of at least 40 shapes is refused in 100% of cases. (d) Run time stays under a stated budget (for example 5 minutes at load below 8, load printed). **Size M.**

### R4-5. Human-queue service levels, error budget and change-freeze policy records
**Idea.** A versioned policy record, owner-set and only able to tighten, with: for each human queue (approvals by tier, task reviews, proposals), a response-time target (for example, 95% of requests have a first decision within N business hours, with D3's one business day as the starting reference for the lowest tier) and a window (28 days, as in D1). The budget is 1 minus the target; a computed burn view (from c58 timestamps, and R3-10's metrics when they land) shows used, left and burn rate with n and "not enough evidence" under a floor. A freeze policy states what a spent budget does: not an automatic block, but a recorded `freeze` row (scope, reason, start, expiry set from DB `now()`, exempt classes: security fix, incident response, owner override with reason) that existing validations read (R4-3 uses the same table). D1's rules become data: freeze on exhaustion, listed exceptions, a required review when one event uses more than 20% of the window, and a named person for disputes. D2's counterpoint becomes a field: the freeze can be recorded as `advisory` (shown, not enforced) or `enforced`, chosen by the owner, with advisory as the default.
**Sources.** D1 (freeze, exceptions, 20% rule, dispute owner), D2 (a spent budget is a decision to discuss, so advisory mode), D3 (a response-time target on a human queue exists in practice; first response may be an acknowledgement), C3 (changes to the system during an investigation are frozen, tying to R4-3). Vendor claims that budget burn can tighten agent autonomy are UNVERIFIED and not used.
**Why for AXION.** The queue and latency metrics of R3-10 and the attention queue of R3-9 measure and display; nothing yet states the target, so no number can ever be "missed". The owner already asked what values the restructure guardrails (Q15) should take; this gives the same kind of knobs for queues, with the same off-by-default stance. It also gives the incident freeze (R4-3) one table to live in.
**Boundary.** Records, a computed view and validation reading the freeze table. Nothing is paged, rolled back or blocked automatically unless an admin set `enforced`. No new autonomy input (Q13 stays open): the budget never changes an agent's tier or grant.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) The burn calculation matches a hand-computed value on at least 8 fixtures, including boundary values (exactly at target, one over), empty windows (null, not 100%) and windows with fewer than the floor of decisions. (b) With an `enforced` freeze, 100% of a fixture matrix of non-exempt change actions are refused with a reason code, and 100% of exempt classes pass; `advisory` never refuses. (c) A freeze expires at its DB-clock expiry in a fixture where the app clock is shifted by a day (no app-clock bound anywhere). (d) A freeze can be tightened by the owner but a lower target cannot be set by a non-owner. (e) Every create, lift and override writes an audit row. **Size M.**

### R4-6. Migration guard for append-only audited tables: manifest, lint and chain proof
**Idea.** A migration manifest and a lint, run in CI and before any live schema change. Each migration file touching a guarded table (any of the append-only tables) must carry a manifest header: which tables, what kind of change (additive, backfill, contract), the guard it needs to bypass if any, and its reverse. The lint refuses: an UPDATE or DELETE on a guarded table without a declared bypass, dropping or weakening a guard trigger, changing a column that feeds the chain hash, and a contract step in the same migration as the expand step. A runner script records `before` and `after` evidence: chain verify passes, chain head, row counts per table and a hash of the immutable columns, and writes a `migration.applied` audit row with both heads. The rule for changes is E2's: evolve how rows are read (schema-version column, default values, read-time conversion) and never rewrite stored rows; structural changes use a new table or chain version with a copy step, in the E1 order of expand, backfill, verify, contract, keeping rollback possible until the contract step.
**Sources.** E1 (expand, backfill, verify, contract; old and new versions live together; rollback before complete), E2 (stored events immutable, conversion at read time, structural change needs a separate transformation), D1 (changes cause most outages; the source states roughly 70%, on one company's data from 2018, so it motivates, not proves), C3 (do not alter systems under investigation without recording it).
**Why for AXION.** AXION's guards are the thing that makes records trustworthy, and a routine migration is the likeliest thing to weaken them (the four-guards rule and the `purge_demo` exception already show this is a recurring hazard). R4-2 will need exactly one such migration, so the lint and proof should land first or with it.
**Boundary.** CI lint, a read-only evidence script and an audit row. It does not run migrations on the live system and does not need a deploy; live use stays an owner-approved step.
**Threshold sketch.** (a) The lint flags 100% of at least 12 seeded bad migrations (rewrite of a guarded row, guard drop, guard weakened, hash-column change, expand and contract in one file, missing manifest, missing reverse) and 0 of the existing migrations after a one-time manifest backfill (report the false-positive count). (b) On a 10,000-row fixture, a seeded additive migration plus a read-time conversion leaves `verify` passing before and after and the immutable-column hash equal; a seeded rewrite makes both checks fail. (c) The evidence script runs in under a stated budget and its output is attached to the audit row (test). **Size S.**
